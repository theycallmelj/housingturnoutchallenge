How this baseline is built

  1. County by county. A statistical model forecasts each county's building permits for 2026-2030 from its own recent permits, its population, how many people are moving in or out, house-price growth and interest rates. The county forecasts are added up to the state.
  2. Permits turn into homes over time. Census data on how long construction takes show that most single-family homes finish within two years of their permit, while apartment buildings often take three years or more, and about a quarter of apartment permits never start.
  3. Checked against history.

Data (all public domain): Census Building Permits Survey, Census Population Estimates, American Community Survey, Census Survey of Construction, FHFA House Price Index, U.S. Treasury yields. Method and limits: MODEL.md.

How we tested the model

We went back in time. For each year from 2015 to 2024 we refitted the baseline model using only the data available that year, had it forecast the next one to five years, and compared the forecasts with the permits actually issued. Every year from 2016 to 2025 was forecast before the model saw it. We scored the same forecasts against two simple rules anyone could use: repeat last year's permits, and repeat the average of the last five years.

Forecasts vs. what actually happened

COVID-19 distorted 2020 and 2021. Permits dipped in spring 2020, then surged in 2021 as mortgage rates hit record lows and people moved out of big cities. No forecast made from earlier years could have seen that coming, and forecasts made from those years started from unusual levels. With "Leave out COVID years" on, those forecasts are greyed out here and left out of the error scores below. Five-year totals can't be separated, because every five-year window from 2016 to 2025 includes 2020 or 2021. Not every spike is COVID: Pennsylvania's 2021 jump, for example, came mostly from Philadelphia builders filing ahead of a property-tax abatement change.

    Limits of this test

    • We used today's revised Census data. A forecaster in 2018 would have seen earlier, slightly different numbers.
    • Forecasts hold interest rates, migration and price growth at their start-year levels, so they could not see the 2021 surge or the 2022 rate rise coming. That is the honest test of a forecast; the sliders above let you explore other conditions.
    • Connecticut is tested for start years 2015-2019 only: Census replaced its counties with planning regions in 2022, so there is no consistent county history after that.
    • The reform scenarios themselves can't be back-tested this way: most states haven't passed these reforms, so there is no "actual" to compare with. Their assumptions come from the studies cited in each scenario instead.
    • Reproduce it: cargo run --release --manifest-path agents/Cargo.toml --bin housing-fit -- --check refits every year and fails if any number differs from what's shown here. CI runs it on every change.
